An MIT ad hoc committee on artificial intelligence in teaching, learning and research training concluded in a report released Tuesday that AI can already "produce credible solutions and provide reasonable responses to almost any written assignment" in the university's undergraduate curriculum.

The committee, made up of faculty, staff and undergraduate and graduate students, urged MIT to let departments make AI-aware curriculum changes regularly rather than through year-long, multi-committee review. It cautioned against relying on AI-detection software, which it called imperfect and said can unfairly affect neurodivergent students and non-native English speakers.

Among its other recommendations: have students write some early project work by hand in class, set regular deadlines with feedback at each stage, avoid capping the number of top grades instructors may give, and be transparent when instructors use AI for course content, feedback or grading.

The report found students use AI "frequently and pervasively" with mixed feelings, while instructors are divided, with some relying on it and others practicing "AI refusal." Over three years, AI use has coincided with fewer students attending office hours, less online discussion and fewer in-person study groups, it said. MIT President Sally Kornbluth called the moment a "watershed moment for MIT" in a campus letter. The committee disclosed its own limited use of AI tools but said none of the final report's text was AI-generated.
