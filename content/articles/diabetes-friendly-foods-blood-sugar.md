There is no single diabetes diet, but nutrition guidelines from groups such as the American Diabetes Association consistently emphasize the same building blocks: plenty of non-starchy vegetables, fiber-rich carbohydrates, lean protein and healthy fats.

**Non-starchy vegetables.** Leafy greens, broccoli, peppers, cauliflower and tomatoes are low in carbohydrates and high in fiber. Filling half your plate with them is a simple, widely recommended approach.

**Beans and lentils.** Legumes combine protein and fiber, which slows digestion and tends to produce a smaller rise in blood sugar than refined carbohydrates.

**Whole grains.** Choices like oats, barley, quinoa and whole-grain bread contain more fiber than white bread or white rice. Portion size still matters, because all carbohydrates raise blood sugar.

**Fish.** Fatty fish such as salmon provide omega-3 fats that support heart health, which is especially important because diabetes raises cardiovascular risk. Many guidelines suggest fish at least twice a week.

**Nuts and seeds.** A small handful of unsalted nuts adds healthy fats and protein.

**Foods and drinks to limit.** Sugary drinks, sweets, refined snacks and large portions of white rice, pasta and bread are the most likely to cause sharp spikes.

Consistency helps: many people find it easier to manage blood sugar by eating meals at regular times and keeping carbohydrate portions similar from day to day. Because needs vary with medication, activity and other conditions, a registered dietitian or diabetes educator can tailor a plan.

*This article is general wellness information, not medical advice. Talk with your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing your diet, especially if you have a medical condition or take medication.*
